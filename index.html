International Capital Markets

International capital markets are interconnected global financial networks where governments, corporations, and institutional investors buy and sell long-term financial instruments such as equities, bonds, and derivatives across national borders.

They facilitate the efficient flow of capital from surplus to deficit regions.

Key Functions & Purpose

  • Capital Sourcing: Allow companies and governments to raise funds outside their domestic borders, often accessing larger pools of capital or securing lower borrowing costs.
  • Risk Diversification: Enable investors to spread risk across different geographies and asset classes, protecting portfolios from localized economic downturns
  • Global Liquidity: Guarantee that funds move freely to locations where they are most efficiently utilized, supporting global economic growth and development

The Gold standard, 1870-1914

In the early days, gold was used as a storage of wealth and as a medium of exchange.

The fundamental principle: Each country should set a par value for its currency in terms of gold.

The exchange rate between any two currencies was determined by their gold content.

  • The first universally implemented system for valuing currencies.
  • Promoted by banks of England.
  • Adopted by UK in 1821, Germany in 1875, France in 1878, U. S. in 1879, Russia in 1897.
  • Lasted for about 40 years as an international monetary system.

For example

if the dollar is pegged to gold at $30 = 1 ounce of gold, and the British pound is pegged to gold at £6 = 1 ounce of gold, the exchange rate is determined by the relative gold contents:

$30 = £6   ⇒   $5 = £1

Meaning

The gold standard is a monetary system in which the value of currencies were pegged to gold at a fixed rate. With the gold standard, countries agree to convert paper money into a fixed amount of gold.

The key elements of the gold standard are;

  1. The government of each country defines its national monetary unit in terms of gold.
  2. Only gold to be used for international payments.
  3. Free import or export of gold.
  4. Two-way convertibility between gold and national currencies. (A country's currency can be exchanged for gold and vice versa at a fixed rate.)

The above conditions were met during the period 1870 to 1914.

Features of Gold Standards System

  • Each country was required to establish a central bank with an exclusive authority to print and issue their respective currency notes.
  • The country's gold reserves were required to be in the custody of the central bank.
  • Each central bank was required to announce an official price of gold
  • Each currency note was required to contain an irrevocable promise to redeem the note on demand against specified quantity of gold.
  • The total value of currency notes in circulation was required to be limited to the value of gold with the central bank.

Advantages of gold standard

  1. Simple monetary standard.
  2. It received positive views of the public much more than any other standard.
  3. Price Stability (Gold forms the currency base and the prices of gold do not fluctuate much)
  4. Exchange Rate stability. (The value of each country's currency was directly tied to a specific amount of gold, and currencies could be freely exchanged for gold at fixed rates)
  5. Trade balance and automatic adjustment mechanism.
  6. Gold standard cannot be secretly tempered with by the independent will of the government.

Disadvantages of gold standard

  1. Not always Simple
  2. One serious defect in this system was that it worked smoothly in the period of peace, and prosperity while in the period of war and economic crises it has always failed.
  3. Gold standard is an expensive standard and a luxury which all the countries cannot afford because a lot of precious metal is wasted.
  4. Under Gold standard the automatic working of the economic system is considered as a demerit.
  5. In the Gold standard independent monetary policy is adopted.

2. The Inter-war Years, 1914-1944

The time between World War I and World War II, from 1915 to 1944.

Currency depreciations: Countries used currency depreciations to gain an advantage in the export market.

  • Exchange rates become unstable.
  • International trade becomes uncertain.
  • Countries often add tariffs and import restrictions to protect domestic industries.
  • Global trade contracts.

Attempts to restore the gold standard: Countries tried to restore the gold standard, but lacked the political will.

U. S. became the world's dominant financial power: The U.S. replaced Britain as the world's dominant financial power.

Sterilization of gold: Mechanism a central bank offsets the impact of gold inflows or outflows on the domestic money supply, preventing the automatic adjustment mechanism of the gold standard from working. (Gold moves, but the money supply does not.)

Bretton Woods System (1945 to 1973)

The Bretton Woods system was an international monetary agreement to revitalize international trade by standardizing exchange rates across the globe.

  • BWS: An international monetary exchange arrangement.
  • The system fixed currencies belonging to 44 countries against the value of the US dollar.
  • The US dollar itself was pegged against the price of gold.
  • Initially, one ounce of gold was worth $35.

On July 01, 1944, 730 delegates from 44 countries attended the United Nations Monetary and Financial Conference held in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire.

Purpose of Conference: Providing consent to a new international monetary system (a collective strategy to recover from the impact of World War II.)

The allied countries duly accepted the Bretton Woods Agreement. (Canada, Mexico, Russia, Brazil, China, India, Netherlands, Poland, Belgium, Chile, and Czechoslovakia were the active member nations).

In December 1945, the BWA led to the formation of two Bretton Woods Institutions

IMF

The International Monetary Fund regulates global exchange rates. (facilitates economic cooperation internationally).

World Bank

The World Bank to help nations recover from World War II. The International Bank for Reconstruction and Development is the lending arm of the World Bank.

Features - BWS

  • Primary Objective: Stabilizing international exchange rates.
  • Attempt to help nations recover economically post-World War II.
  • Bretton Woods was adopted by 44 countries-they agreed to peg their currencies against the USD.
  • The US Dollar was pegged against the price of gold-fixed at $35 per ounce of gold.
  • The US Dollar was considered-an international reserve currency.
  • It provided a fixed exchange rate. However, this rate was adjustable.
  • It standardized international monetary payments by facilitating currency conversion.

The Bretton Woods arrangement failed due to the following reasons

  1. The system depleted US gold reserves-as more and more US dollars were issued to meet international demand.
  2. In the 1960s post-Vietnam War, the US struggled with inflation. Its current account balance was low; therefore, the government decided to discontinue this system.
  3. Lacked proper adjustment mechanism for the balance of payments.
  4. There was a deficit balance of payment in the US. Meanwhile, there was a huge demand for the US dollar worldwide, resulting in liquidity issues.
  5. Finally, in response to inflation and the current account balance deficit, the government declared restrictions on gold-dollar conversions.

Advantages of BWS

  1. Stability and Predictability in Exchange Rates
    • a) Fixed Exchange rate: Currencies were pegged to the U.S. dollar, which itself was tied to gold. Exchange rate stability. Reduce exchange rate volatility in international trade and investment
    • b) Reduced Exchange Rate Risk: Businesses and investors did not have to worry about the fluctuating value of foreign currencies
  2. Promoted International Trade and Economic Growth: (Reconstruction of War-Torn Economies)
  3. Prevented Competitive Devaluations. Exchange Rate Discipline: The fixed exchange rate system prevented countries from engaging in competitive devaluations (where countries deliberately devalue their currencies to boost exports)
  4. U.S. Dollar as a Reserve Currency: The U.S. dollar's peg to gold gave confidence to the international community in its stability, making it the primary global reserve currency.
  5. International Financial Institutions (IMF and World Bank)

Disadvantages of BWS

  1. Dependence on the U.S. Dollar and Gold: Any instability in the U.S. economy, fiscal policy, or monetary policy could create problems for the entire global financial system.
  2. Gold Convertibility Issues: The U.S. faced growing pressure to maintain gold reserves to back the dollar. As global demand for U.S. dollars grew, the U.S. found itself unable to maintain the gold peg, ultimately leading to the collapse of the system.
  3. Rigid Exchange Rate Mechanism: Less flexibility to adjust their exchange rates in response to changing economic conditions.
  4. Unilateral Actions and Lack of Coordination: Nixon Shock (1971): Nixon unilaterally suspended the dollar's convertibility into gold, which undermined the stability of the entire system.
  5. Limits on Developing Countries: Lack of Policy Autonomy: The fixed exchange rate system limited the ability of developing countries to manage their own monetary policies.

Post Bretton Woods breakdown, countries did not need to peg currencies against USD or gold prices.

Flexible Exchange Rate Regime

The exchange rate is not fixed by government authorities rather it is determined by the forces of market i.e. supply and demand of the currencies in the international market).

Free Float System

Under this system, the exchange rate is determined solely by the market forces and it does not involve intervention. Also known as clean float or independent float system.

Managed Float System

A country's monetary authorities interfere directly or indirectly to stabilize the exchange rate and to keep it within desired limits. It is also known as dirty floating.

  • Direct intervention: the monetary authorities attempt to stabilize the exchange rate by purchasing and selling foreign currency in the domestic market.
  • Indirect intervention: the monetary authorities bring changes in the interest rates to stabilize the exchange rates

Crawling Peg System

A synthesis of fixed and floating exchange rate system.

A crawling peg is a system where the government or central bank adjusts the exchange rate gradually and regularly, usually by small amounts, instead of making sudden large devaluations.

Involves periodic adjustment of fixed exchange rate to catch up with the market determined rates.

Fluctuating exchange rate

Refers to the constant changes in the value of a currency relative to another currency or basket of currencies due to market forces of supply and demand in the foreign exchange (forex) market.

It's essentially the opposite of a fixed or pegged exchange rate (the currency's value is tied to another currency, like the U.S. dollar or gold).

Fluctuating exchange rate regime is a broader term: Freely floating currencies + and managed or dirty floats (but still allows the market to influence the rate).

Features of a Fluctuating Exchange Rate

  • Market-Driven
  • Frequent Changes
  • No Government Control (except: Managed float system)
  • Flexibility

FDI, its types, and multilateral organisations

The notes write "types of FDI?" next to the FDI capital channel, and list four types with no explanation. The definitions below are standard textbook ones.

Foreign Direct Investment (FDI): an investment by a company or person in one country into a business in another country that gives a lasting interest and some control over that business. Buying a factory, setting up a subsidiary or buying a large stake (10% or more of voting power, by the IMF/OECD rule) counts as FDI. Buying a small number of shares only for returns is portfolio investment (FPI).

  • Horizontal FDI: the firm sets up the same business abroad that it runs at home. Example: Hyundai making cars in its Chennai plant, as it does in Korea.
  • Vertical FDI: the firm invests abroad in a different stage of its own supply chain. Backward vertical means moving into inputs (a carmaker buying a steel or parts supplier abroad). Forward vertical means moving into distribution or sales (a manufacturer opening its own sales network abroad).
  • Platform FDI: the firm invests in one country mainly to export from there to other countries. Example: a foreign phone maker sets up assembly in India to ship phones to the US and Europe.
  • Conglomerate FDI: the firm invests abroad in a business unrelated to its existing one. Example: a foreign bank buying a hotel chain in another country.

Balance of Payments (BOP): meaning

BOP is a systematic record of all economic transactions between the residents of a country and the rest of the world during a specific period (usually one year).

Economic transactions:

  • Exports and imports of goods and services.
  • Capital inflows and outflows.
  • Gifts and other transfer payments and changes in a country's international reserves.

A transaction enters the BOP only when one side is a resident and the other a non-resident (see Q4, item vii).

BOP statement format

The worked solutions lay the statement out with three columns: Debit (Payments), Credit (Receipts) and Net.

  1. Goods: exports and imports of goods
  2. Services: tourism, medical tourism, software, travel agency bills
  3. Primary income: interest, dividend, share of profit from a joint venture
  4. Secondary income: remittances sent home or abroad, gifts

The notes group primary and secondary income together under "Unilateral transfers".

B. Capital account
  • FDI inflow / outflow
  • FPI (portfolio, e.g. buying bonds)
  • Short-term borrowing
  • Long-term liabilities
C. Official reserve account

Takes the overall balance (A + B) so that the statement closes at zero. One set of notes calls it "Reserve A/c" or "Capital Reserve A/c", with the line "Change in reserves".

  • Overall deficit → decrease in forex reserves, shown on the credit side (withdraw from reserves; if needed, borrow from the IMF)
  • Overall surplus → increase in forex reserves, shown on the debit side (deposit the excess in reserves)

The entries in C are accommodating transactions: the government makes them only to bring the BOP into equilibrium.

Standard contrast: the items in A and B are autonomous transactions, done for their own sake (trade, investment, gifts). Accommodating transactions in C only cover the gap the autonomous ones leave.

Statement of Sources and Uses of foreign exchange

Before the BOP statement, every question first prepares a statement of sources and uses. Each transaction is tagged current a/c or capital a/c and placed in one column:

  • Sources (Receipts): anything that brings foreign exchange in. Exports of goods and services, income received from abroad, remittances into India, foreign investment into India, borrowing from abroad.
  • Uses (Payments): anything that sends foreign exchange out. Imports, interest and dividends paid abroad, remittances out of India, Indian investment abroad.

Net foreign exchange = Total sources − Total uses. A negative figure is a net use of funds (deficit). A positive figure is a net source (surplus).

1. American Depositary Receipts (ADRs)

Negotiable certificates issued by a U.S. depositary bank representing a specified number of shares of a foreign company's (Indian) stock. The ADR trades on U.S. stock markets as any domestic shares would.

ADRs offer U.S. investors a way to purchase stock in overseas companies that would not otherwise be available. Foreign firms (Indian) also benefit, as ADRs enable them to attract American investors and capital without the hassle and expense of listing on U.S. stock exchanges.

The sale and purchase of ADRs can only happen in the capital market of America. These depository receipts are listed only on the stock exchange of the USA and nowhere else.

With the issuance of ADRs, US investors get an opportunity to invest in overseas companies. So this widens the horizons of investing opportunities.

Raising funds by an Indian Company through ADR's

  • Deposits of the securities of an Indian company with a custodian bank (agent of the depository) in India.
  • The custodian bank makes acknowledgement of deposits to the depository in the US.
  • The depository in the US then issues the ADR's to the investors in the US.
  • The US$ receipts in the hands of the depository are passed on to the Indian Company issuing shares.

Features of ADRs (In context of an Indian Company)

  1. ADRs are US$ denominated and are traded in the same way as are the securities of US companies.
  2. The ADR holder is entitled to the same rights and advantages as owners of the shares in India.
  3. ADR represents ownership in shares of Indian Company that is traded in US Stock Exchanges.
  4. ADRs pay dividends in US$ and can be traded like shares of US based companies.
  5. The value of ADR fluctuates based on the price movements of the underlying shares.

2. Global Depository Receipt

The principal difference between ADR and GDR is in the market in which they are issued and the exchange in which they are listed.

A negotiable financial instrument issued by a depositary bank. It represents shares in a foreign company and trades on the local stock exchanges in investors' countries. GDRs make it possible for a company (the issuer) to access investors in capital markets beyond the borders of its own country.

ADR numericals

The issue process is on the slide. The numericals work out how many shares go to the custodian and what each ADR costs:

  1. Amount to be raised in ₹ = US$ amount × exchange rate
  2. Number of shares required = Amount in ₹ / market price of one share
  3. Number of ADRs = Number of shares / shares per ADR (the ADR ratio)
  4. ADR issue price (US$) = (share price in ₹ × shares per ADR) / exchange rate

Currency adjusted return (RCA)

As in Unit 2, the rupee return combines the share return and the currency return:

RCA = (1 + RS)(1 + RC) − 1

  • RS = (P1 − P0) / P0: return from the security, in its own currency
  • RC = (S1 − S0) / S0: return from the exchange rate change (S in ₹ per unit of foreign currency)

If the share price doesn't move, RS = 0 and RCA = RC. Unit 2 Problem 1 is this case.

Beta

Beta measures how much a security's return moves with the market's return.

β = Cov(Ri, Rm)Var(Rm)

Cov(Ri, Rm) = Σ (Ri − R̄i)(Rm − R̄m)n    Var(Rm) = Σ (Rm − R̄m)²n

Ri is the security's return. For a foreign security held by an Indian investor, use the currency adjusted return. Rm is the market index return.

Two other ways to get beta (from the class notes)

(i) Regression method. Fit the straight line y = a + bx, i.e. Ri = α + β Rm:

β = n ΣRmRi − (ΣRm)(ΣRi)n ΣRm² − (ΣRm)²    α = R̄i − β R̄m

where R̄i = ΣRi / n and R̄m = ΣRm / n.

(ii) Correlation method.

β = rim σiσm    rim = Cov(Ri, Rm)σi σm

or, straight from the data:

rim = n ΣRiRm − (ΣRm)(ΣRi)√(n ΣRm² − (ΣRm)²) × √(n ΣRi² − (ΣRi)²)

All three methods give the same beta.

Alpha (α). Even if the market is underperforming, alpha tells us whether the company itself is performing well or not: it is the part of the security's return that the market doesn't explain.

Reading the value (added for context, not in the notes): β > 1 means the security moves more than the market (aggressive). β < 1 means it moves less (defensive). β = 1 means it moves with the market.

Insurance Mechanisms and Climate-Related Disasters

Insurance is a mechanism through which individuals, businesses or governments transfer part of their financial risk to an insurance company in exchange for a premium.

Insurance is particularly important for climate-related disasters because these events can cause very large and sudden financial losses.

Role of Insurance in Reducing Financial Impact

  1. Risk Transfer: Insurance transfers the financial consequences of a disaster from the individual or business to the insurer. Example: If a farmer's crop is destroyed by drought, crop insurance can compensate the farmer for the covered loss.
  2. Financial Protection: Insurance provides funds for rebuilding after disasters. For example:
    • House damaged by a cyclone (property insurance provides compensation).
    • Crops destroyed by floods (crop insurance provides compensation).
    • Business interrupted by a disaster (business-interruption insurance can provide financial support).
  3. Promotes Faster Recovery: Insurance payouts can help households and businesses restart economic activities more quickly after a disaster.
  4. Encourages Risk Reduction: Insurance companies can encourage safer behaviour by:
    • Offering lower premiums for safer properties
    • Requiring safety standards
    • Providing incentives for disaster-resistant infrastructure
  5. Risk Pooling: Insurance works through risk pooling. A large number of policyholders contribute premiums to a common pool. Only some policyholders will suffer losses at a particular time. Therefore, the financial burden of losses is distributed across the pool. Important principle: Risk pooling works best when individual risks are relatively independent.
  6. Supports Government Disaster Management: Insurance can reduce the financial burden on governments by shifting some disaster-recovery costs to private insurers and reinsurance markets.

Types of Climate-Related Insurance

  • Crop Insurance: Protects farmers against specified agricultural losses.
  • Weather-Index Insurance: Payouts are linked to measurable weather conditions rather than actual individual losses. For example, a policy might provide payment when rainfall falls below a predetermined level.
  • Property Insurance: Protects buildings and property against covered risks such as floods, storms and other disasters.
  • Parametric Insurance: Payment is triggered when a predefined event or parameter reaches a specified threshold. Example: If wind speed exceeds a specified level during a cyclone, the insured receives a predetermined payment.
  • Reinsurance: Insurance companies themselves transfer part of their risks to reinsurers. This is particularly important for catastrophic climate events because a single disaster can generate huge claims.

PPP: Purchasing Power Parity

Purchasing Power Parity (PPP) is an economic theory used to compare the absolute purchasing power of different currencies and determine whether a currency is overvalued or undervalued relative to another.

The Core Concept
  • PPP is based on the Law of One Price, which states that in an ideal market with no transaction costs, trade barriers, or transport costs, identical goods should sell for the same price in different countries when priced in a common currency.
  • Instead of using individual goods, PPP compares the price of a standardized basket of goods and services across different countries.

PPP Exchange Rate = Price of Basket in Country 1 (Local Currency)Price of Basket in Country 2 (Foreign Currency)

The Big Mac Index (A Popular Example)

Created by The Economist as an informal illustration of PPP

The Big Mac Index compares the price of a McDonald's Big Mac burger across countries to determine currency over- or undervaluation relative to the US Dollar:

Example: If a Big Mac costs $5.00 in the US and ₹200 in India, the PPP implied exchange rate is:

= ₹200 / $5.00 = ₹40/US$

If the actual market exchange rate is ₹80/US$, the Indian Rupee is considered 50% undervalued relative to the US Dollar according to the Big Mac Index.

Limitations of PPP

  • Non-Tradable Goods & Services: Services like haircuts, housing, or healthcare cannot be easily traded internationally, leading to persistent price differences.
  • Trade Barriers & Transport Costs: Tariffs, taxes, shipping costs, and import restrictions prevent prices from equalizing globally.
  • Differences in Basket Quality & Consumer Preferences: The exact same basket of goods is rarely consumed with identical quality or preference patterns across different countries.

Relative Purchasing Power Parity

  • Relative Purchasing Power Parity (Relative PPP) is an extension of Absolute PPP. While Absolute PPP states that exchange rates equal the ratio of price levels between two countries, Relative PPP focuses on changes over time.
  • It asserts that the change in the exchange rate between two currencies over a given period is determined by the difference in their national inflation rates during that period.
Key Concept

If Country A experiences higher inflation than Country B, Country A's currency should depreciate relative to Country B's currency by approximately the amount of the inflation differential. This mechanism ensures that foreign and domestic goods maintain equal relative purchasing power over time.

Interest Rate Parity (IRP)

"With PPP, we said that a Big Mac or a basket of goods should cost the same whether you buy it in Delhi or New York. But global investors aren't just buying burgers-they're moving billions of dollars into bank deposits and bonds to earn interest. Interest Rate Parity (IRP) says that the returns on risk-free investments in two different currencies must yield the exact same return once exchange rate risk is factored in."

Interest Rate Parity (IRP) is a fundamental theory in international finance that states that the interest rate differential between two countries is equal to the differential between the forward exchange rate and the spot exchange rate.

In simple terms, IRP asserts that an investor should earn the same total return whether they invest in a domestic bank account or convert their currency to invest in a foreign bank account (after hedging against currency risk). It creates a "no-arbitrage" condition-meaning there is no "free lunch" or risk-free profit to be made simply by moving money to a country with higher interest rates

Role of Technology in Global Financial Markets: Fintech and Cryptocurrency

Technology has shifted global financial markets from centralized, floor-traded models to hyper-connected, digital ecosystems. Financial technology (Fintech), Artificial Intelligence (AI), Distributed Ledger Technology (DLT), and cloud architecture have altered capital allocation, cross-border payment flows, and market liquidity.

This transformation impacts market participants, regulatory agencies, retail investors, and sovereign financial institutions alike.

This transition reshapes capital mobility, market liquidity, operational structures, and the regulatory perimeter across international borders.

Core Technological Drivers in Global Capital Markets

  • High-Frequency and Algorithmic Trading: Computer algorithms execute complex, cross-border trades in microsecond intervals. High-frequency trading (HFT) provides market liquidity and narrows bid-ask spreads. However, HFT can also cause flash crashes when automated strategies withdraw liquidity simultaneously during volatile periods.
  • Artificial Intelligence and Alternative Data: Asset managers use machine learning to process structured financial statements alongside unstructured alternative data (such as global supply chain telemetry, satellite tracking, and real-time transaction feeds) to evaluate asset values and system-wide risks.
  • Cloud Computing and Open Banking APIs: API connections enable interoperability between traditional banking institutions, capital market exchanges, and third-party software platforms, simplifying cross-border payment flows and clearing processes.

Fintech Disruptions in International Finance

  • Cross-Border Payments and Remittances: Real-time payment networks and digital clearing systems bypass traditional multi-tiered correspondent banking arrangements, reducing cross-border transfer friction, settlement latency, and exchange fees.
  • Robo-Advisory and Automated Wealth Management: Digital wealth management platforms democratize asset allocation by automating portfolio construction, risk profiling, and tax-loss harvesting for both retail and institutional investors.
  • Alternative Financing and Digital Crowdfunding: Peer-to-peer (P2P) lending platforms and digital equity crowdfunding connect global borrowers directly with capital providers, bypassing corporate banking desks.

Cryptocurrencies, Blockchain, and Decentralized Finance (DeFi)

Cryptographic assets and public ledgers represent a shift toward decentralized, programmatic settlement systems in international financial architecture.

  • Layer-1 Protocols and Distributed Ledger Technology (DLT): Blockchain networks record transactions across decentralized nodes using consensus algorithms, ensuring immutability without central clearing authorities.
  • Stablecoins and Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs): Fiat-backed tokens maintain dollar or multi-currency parity, providing settlement rails for cross-border transactions without traditional bank wire delays.
  • Decentralized Finance (DeFi): Autonomous, smart-contract-based platforms facilitate peer-to-peer lending, borrowing, and token trading, eliminating intermediary administrative costs while introducing code-level operational risks.
  • Tokenization of Real-World Assets (RWA): Fractional ownership of illiquid global assets, such as commercial real estate, private debt, and Treasury bills, on public or permissioned blockchains increases secondary market liquidity.

Ethical and Sustainable Decision-Making in International Investment Management

International investment management has expanded beyond the single objective of risk-adjusted financial returns to incorporate broader ethical and sustainable considerations. Global asset managers operate under shifting regulatory frameworks, varying cultural norms, and heightened stakeholder scrutiny.

Integrating ethical principles into global capital allocation ensures that fiduciary duty encompasses long-term risk management, environmental stewardship, and social accountability across jurisdictions.

Core Drivers of Ethical and Sustainable Investing

  • Evolution of Fiduciary Duty: Modern legal interpretations emphasize that ignoring long-term environmental, social, and governance (ESG) risks can constitute a breach of fiduciary duty, as these factors directly impact systemic financial performance.
  • Regulatory Mandates: Regulators globally enforce strict disclosures to combat greenwashing (e.g., the EU Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation, SFDR), requiring asset managers to substantiate sustainability claims with standardized data.
  • Demographic and Institutional Demand: Sovereign wealth funds, pension funds, and a new generation of retail investors increasingly direct capital toward funds that align with personal values, corporate governance standards, and global decarbonization targets.

  • Impact Investing: Directing capital into projects or enterprises designed to generate measurable social or environmental solutions alongside competitive financial returns (e.g., clean infrastructure, affordable housing, green bonds).
  • Active Stewardship and Proxy Voting: Engaging directly with corporate management and using shareholder voting power to influence board diversity, carbon reduction targets, and executive compensation structures.

Issues of Data Privacy and Cybersecurity in Digital Investing

The digital transformation of investment management, spanning cloud platforms, automated trading algorithms, open banking APIs, and fintech apps, has expanded the attack surface for global capital markets.

Digital investing platforms handle sensitive institutional trade data, proprietary market algorithms, and investors' Personally Identifiable Information (PII). Protecting this infrastructure requires balancing real-time data flows with strict compliance standards and robust cybersecurity controls.

Data Privacy Challenges

  • Cross-Border Regulatory Compliance: Global investment firms must navigate conflicting privacy regimes, such as the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA), and India's Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act.   • Consent Management and Data Sovereignty: Digital wealth platforms collect user telemetry, risk preferences, and banking records. Ensuring explicit consent for data processing and retaining sovereign ownership over client profiles are major legal liabilities.
  • Alternative Data and Unintended Tracking: Quantitative hedge funds and asset managers increasingly use alternative datasets (such as geolocation logs and credit card transaction feeds). Cybersecurity Threats in Digital Investing

Climate Change, Social Responsibility, and Governance as Investment Factors

Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) factors have transitioned from secondary non-financial metrics into fundamental drivers of international investment evaluation and risk management. Institutional investors incorporate these variables alongside quantitative financial metrics to price physical and transition risks, protect long-term capital, and capture emerging market opportunities during the global energy transition.

Climate Change as a Strategic Investment Factor

Climate change influences corporate valuation through two distinct risk categories and a growing set of transition opportunities.

Risk Categories
  • Physical Risks: Direct operational and asset losses caused by acute weather events (e.g., floods, wildfires, hurricanes) or chronic shifts (e.g., rising sea levels, prolonged heatwaves). These risks directly impact real estate, agriculture, insurance, and supply chain logistics.
  • Transition Risks: Financial and regulatory pressures resulting from the shift toward a low-carbon economy.
    • Policy & Legal Risks: Carbon pricing mechanisms, emissions caps, and mandatory disclosure requirements (e.g., TCFD/ISSB standards).
    • Technology Risks: Stranded asset risks where fossil-fuel infrastructure or high-carbon operational models become economically obsolete.
    • Market Risks: Shifting consumer and institutional demand toward low-emission goods and services.
Measurement Frameworks
  • Carbon Accounting: Calculating Scope 1 (direct operations), Scope 2 (purchased energy), and Scope 3 (value chain) emissions to assess a firm's carbon intensity.
  • Scenario Analysis & Stress Testing: Modeling corporate cash flows under various global warming pathways (e.g., 1.5°C vs. 2°C+ climate trajectories).

Social Responsibility as an Investment Factor

Social factors evaluate how a enterprise manages relationships with its workforce, suppliers, customers, and local communities across its global footprint.

  • Human Capital Management: Assessing workforce retention, labor relations, safety records, and diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) initiatives. Strong human capital practices correlate with lower operational disruption and higher productivity.
  • Supply Chain Ethics and Human Rights: Evaluating multi-tier supply chains for forced labor, child labor, and unsafe working conditions, particularly in global manufacturing and extraction industries.
  • Consumer Protection and Data Safety: Ensuring customer data privacy, cybersecurity resilience, and product safety to avoid reputational damage and regulatory penalties.
  • Community Relations and Social License to Operate: Managing local stakeholder impact, indigenous land rights, and regional employment when executing large infrastructure or resource extraction projects.

Governance as an Investment Factor

Corporate governance serves as the structural foundation for enforcing environmental and social accountability. It measures internal oversight mechanisms and management incentives.

  • Board Independence and Diversity: Ensuring non-executive, independent directors oversee executive decision-making and mitigate conflicts of interest.
  • Executive Compensation Alignment: Structuring executive pay structures to tie bonuses directly to long-term performance and sustainability metrics, rather than short-term earnings targets.
  • Shareholder Rights: Preserving proportional voting rights ("one share, one vote") and limiting anti-takeover protections that insulate management from investor accountability.
  • Business Ethics and Compliance: Enforcing strict policies against bribery, corruption, tax evasion, and anti-competitive practices across global subsidiaries.

Q5. Gold standard or sterilization (5 marks)

Show answer

  • Gold for international payments: only gold was used to settle international payments.
  • Free movement: gold could be freely imported and exported.
  • Two-way convertibility: currency could be exchanged for gold and gold for currency at a fixed rate.

b) Evaluation

  • Why it blocks adjustment: under the gold standard a deficit country lost gold, its money supply and prices fell and its exports recovered. Sterilization cut this link, so imbalances stayed.
  • Supporting the statement: in the inter-war years countries sterilized gold to protect their domestic economies, and the automatic correction stopped working.
  • Other causes too: competitive currency depreciations, tariffs and import restrictions, unstable exchange rates and falling world trade also weakened the system.
  • Lack of political will: countries tried to restore the gold standard but lacked the political will to make it work. The US also replaced Britain as the dominant financial power, so the old London-centred system could not simply return.
  • Conclusion: the statement is largely true. Sterilization removed the core self-correcting mechanism, but it was one of several reasons the system failed.

Q1. ADR issue process (5 marks)

Explain the process by which an Indian company raises funds from US investors through American Depositary Receipts (ADRs).

Show answer

Indian company shares are not listed directly on US exchanges. They reach US investors through ADRs. The aim is raising funds from overseas.

Show answer

Q9. PPP concept and limits (5 marks)

a) Explain the concept of Purchasing Power Parity and the Law of One Price. Indicate how the Big Mac Index illustrates this idea.

Or

b) "Exchange rates rarely settle at the level suggested by Purchasing Power Parity." Assess the limitations of PPP that explain this.

Show answer
a) PPP and the Law of One Price
  • PPP: a theory that compares the purchasing power of currencies and tells whether a currency is overvalued or undervalued.
  • Law of One Price: with no transaction costs, trade barriers or transport costs, identical goods should sell for the same price in different countries when priced in a common currency.
  • Basket: PPP uses a standard basket of goods and services instead of a single good.

PPP rate = Price of basket in Country 1 (local currency) ÷ Price of basket in Country 2 (foreign currency)

  • Big Mac Index: created by The Economist as an informal PPP test. It compares the price of one Big Mac across countries against the US dollar.
  • Reading it: if the PPP rate from Big Mac prices is lower than the market rate (in ₹ per $), the rupee is undervalued; if higher, the rupee is overvalued.
b) Limitations of PPP
  • Non-tradable goods and services: haircuts, housing and healthcare cannot be traded across borders, so price gaps persist.
  • Trade barriers and transport costs: tariffs, taxes, shipping costs and import limits stop prices from equalising.
  • Basket quality and preferences: the same basket is rarely consumed with the same quality or taste in every country.
  • Long-term theory: PPP works through slow price adjustment in goods markets, so it explains long-run trends better than day-to-day rates.
  • Conclusion: PPP is a useful benchmark for over or undervaluation, not an exact forecast.

Q1. Fintech disruptions (5 marks)

Describe the major fintech disruptions in international finance with suitable examples.

Show answer

Fintech means using technology (apps, APIs, cloud, AI) to deliver financial services. It has changed how money moves across borders in three main ways.

Q2. HFT: liquidity vs fragility (5 marks)

"High-frequency trading makes global markets more liquid, but it can also make them more fragile." Evaluate.

Show answer

Meaning: High-frequency trading (HFT) is algorithmic trading where computers place and cancel cross-border orders in microseconds.

Why it adds liquidity (supports the statement)

  • More orders: HFT firms quote buy and sell prices all the time, so there is usually someone on the other side of a trade.
  • Narrower bid-ask spreads: competition among algorithms reduces the gap between buy and sell prices, which lowers trading cost for every investor.
  • Faster price discovery: new information is reflected in prices across markets almost instantly.

Why it adds fragility (also supports the statement)

  • Liquidity can vanish: in volatile periods many automated strategies stop trading at the same time, so the liquidity disappears exactly when it is needed.
  • Flash crashes: this sudden withdrawal can cause prices to fall sharply within minutes and then recover.
  • Data and code risk: algorithms can be misled by bad or poisoned input data.

Conclusion: the statement is valid. HFT improves liquidity in normal times, but that liquidity is unreliable under stress, so regulators and exchanges need safeguards.

Q4. Blockchain, stablecoins, DeFi (5 marks)

Explain how Distributed Ledger Technology, stablecoins and CBDCs, and Decentralized Finance are changing international financial settlement.

Show answer
  • Distributed Ledger Technology (DLT): a blockchain records every transaction across many decentralised nodes. Nodes agree through a consensus algorithm.
  • Immutability: once recorded, entries cannot be changed, so trust comes from the network and not from a central clearing house.
  • Stablecoins: tokens backed by fiat money that keep parity with the dollar or a basket of currencies. They give a settlement rail for cross-border payments without bank wire delays.
  • CBDCs: digital currency issued by a central bank (for example, RBI's digital rupee, e₹). It is a sovereign version of the same idea.
  • DeFi: smart-contract platforms that allow peer-to-peer lending, borrowing and token trading without intermediaries, which cuts administrative cost.
  • The trade-off: DeFi removes the middleman but adds code-level risks such as smart-contract bugs, flash-loan exploits and algorithmic de-pegging of stablecoins.

Q7. Ethical decision challenges (5 marks)

Unit 1

Role of institutional investors

Unit 1 already lists the types and says they provide capital and diversify. Missing part: what role they play.

  • Long-term money: pension funds and insurers hold assets for decades, so they can fund long projects such as roads and power plants.

Challenges in cross-border investments

Investing in another country is harder than investing at home. Apart from the risks listed in Unit 1, the investor faces practical problems:

  • Different laws, taxes and accounting standards in each country.
  • Less information about foreign companies (information gap).
  • Capital controls and limits on how much money can go out or come in.
  • Higher costs: brokerage, currency conversion, custody fees.
  • Language, culture and time-zone differences.

Example: An Indian who owns US shares has US tax cut from each dividend before it arrives, and the same dividend is taxed again in India. The India-US tax treaty (DTAA) lets the investor claim credit for the US tax so the investor is not taxed twice in full.

Capital flows

Unit 1 already lists the channels (FDI, portfolio investment, bank lending, VC and private markets) and the BOP notes cover the capital account. Missing part:

Capital flows are movements of money across borders for investment. Inflows come into a country; outflows leave it. FDI is stable, long-term money. Portfolio money is called "hot money" because it can leave quickly. Flows depend on pull factors in the receiving country (high growth, good returns) and push factors in the investor's own country (low interest rates there).

Example: When the US Federal Reserve raises interest rates, FPIs often pull money out of India to earn more in the US. The rupee falls and Indian share prices dip.

Global financial linkages

Global financial linkages are the connections between financial markets of different countries. Countries are linked through trade, capital flows, banks lending across borders, exchange rates and common investors. Because of these links, a shock in one country spreads to others. This spread is called contagion.

Example: In 2008 the US investment bank Lehman Brothers collapsed. Foreign investors pulled money out of emerging markets, and the Sensex fell by more than half from its January 2008 peak, though the crisis started in the US.

Impact of macroeconomic indicators (interest rates, inflation, GDP) on investment decisions

Unit 1 names these as economic factors, and Unit 2 (Global Equities, question bank Q7) gives their effect on share prices. Missing part: how they change other investment decisions.

  • Interest rates up: new bonds and FDs pay more, so money shifts from shares to debt.
  • Inflation up: the central bank raises rates, so the above follows. Investors prefer gold or property, which hold value.
  • GDP growth up: investors favour cyclical sectors like autos and banks, whose sales rise fastest in a boom.

Example: From May 2022 to February 2023 RBI raised the repo rate from 4% to 6.5% to control inflation. FD rates rose, so many savers moved money into FDs.

Concept of sustainable investing

Sustainable investing means choosing investments by looking at both financial return and their effect on the environment and society. Investors check ESG factors (environmental, social, governance) along with profits. The methods (SRI, ESG integration, impact investing) are in Unit 5.

Example: An investor puts money in an ESG index fund that tracks the Nifty100 ESG index, which gives more weight to companies with better ESG scores, instead of a plain index fund.

Role of investment in climate change

Fighting climate change needs huge amounts of money, and investment supplies it. It works in two ways:

  • Cutting emissions: money for solar and wind plants, electric vehicles, green hydrogen.
  • Adaptation: money for coping with climate damage, like the Odisha and Netherlands cases in Unit 4.

Unit 4 uses "mitigation" more widely, for any step that reduces climate risk.

Example: India issued its first sovereign green bonds in January and February 2023, raising ₹16,000 crore in two parts of ₹8,000 crore each, for projects such as renewable energy and clean transport.

Unit 2

Mutual funds

A mutual fund pools money from many investors and a professional fund manager invests it in shares, bonds or money market instruments. Investors get units, and the value of one unit is the NAV (net asset value). Main types are equity, debt, hybrid, index funds and ETFs. In India they are regulated by SEBI.

Example: You invest ₹10,000 in a fund when its NAV is ₹50, so you get 200 units. If NAV rises to ₹60, your units are worth ₹12,000. Investing a fixed sum every month is called a SIP.

Participants and functions (of derivatives markets)

Participants:

  • Hedgers: use derivatives to reduce risk. A wheat farmer sells wheat futures to lock in a price.
  • Speculators: take risk to earn profit from price moves. A trader buys Nifty futures expecting the index to rise.
  • Arbitrageurs: make riskless profit from price differences between two markets (worked example in the next card).

Functions (besides the hedging and price discovery on the slides): risk moves from hedgers to speculators who are willing to carry it, trading becomes more liquid, and costs are lower because only a margin is paid, not the full value.

Valuation problems

Unit 2 already has the hedge savings and return numericals. Missing part: pricing a forward or future.

A forward price is found by the cost of carry model: the spot price plus the interest cost of holding the asset until delivery.

F = S × (1 + r)t

S = spot price, r = interest rate per year, t = time in years. If the asset pays a dividend before delivery, its present value is subtracted from S first.

Example: A share trades at ₹1,000. Interest rate is 8% a year. Six-month forward price:
F = 1,000 × (1.08)0.5 = ₹1,039.23.
If the market quotes ₹1,060, the future is overpriced: buy the share, sell the future, and gain ₹20.77.

Unit 3

Risk and Uncertainty

Risk is the chance that the actual return will differ from the expected return. In risk, the possible outcomes and their probabilities are known, so risk can be measured (for example, by standard deviation, σ). Uncertainty is when the outcomes or their probabilities are not known, so it cannot be measured.

Example: A share's past returns show a 60% chance of 12% return and a 40% chance of 4%. That is risk. Whether a new government policy next year will help or hurt the company, with no data to go on, is uncertainty.

Causes for risk

Returns vary for many reasons. The Unit 1 risk list (exchange rate, interest rate, political, regulatory, liquidity, credit) already names most outside causes. Others are:

  • Outside the company: inflation, recession, market sentiment and investor mood.
  • Inside the company: wrong business decisions or poor management (business risk), and heavy borrowing (financial risk).

Example: When sales dip by the same amount, a company with heavy borrowing sees its profit fall much more than a debt-free rival, because its interest bill stays fixed (financial risk).

Types of Risk: Systematic and Unsystematic risk

Systematic risk (market risk) affects the whole market. It comes from the outside causes in the card above. It cannot be removed by diversification and is measured by beta (β).

Unsystematic risk (specific risk) affects only one company or industry and comes from the inside causes. It can be reduced by holding many different shares.

Total risk = Systematic risk + Unsystematic risk

Example: The COVID crash in March 2020 pulled almost all shares down (systematic). A fire at one pharma company's plant hurts only that company (unsystematic).

Expected risk return trade-off

The risk-return trade-off says that higher expected return comes only with higher risk. A safe investment pays a low return; to earn more, the investor must accept more ups and downs. The return on a riskless asset is the risk-free rate, and the extra return for taking risk is the risk premium.

Example: An Indian government bond may give about 6 to 7% with almost no risk. Small-cap shares may give 15% or more in a good year but can also lose 30% in a bad year.

Portfolio management process

Portfolio management is done in steps:

  1. Set objectives and constraints: return needed, risk the investor can bear, time period, tax, liquidity needs.
  2. Asset allocation: decide how much goes to equity, debt, gold, foreign assets.
  3. Security selection: pick the actual shares, bonds or funds.
  4. Execution: buy the investments.
  5. Monitoring and revision: review and rebalance when markets or needs change.
  6. Performance evaluation: compare returns with a benchmark.

Example: A 25-year-old saving for 20 years chooses 80% equity and 20% debt, picks index funds, and rebalances once a year.

International portfolio diversification

Unit 3 covers currency adjusted return. Missing part: why spreading across countries cuts risk.

Each country's market has its own risks: its economy, its policies, its business cycle. These do not hit all countries at the same time, so their returns have low correlation and in a mixed portfolio the country risks partly cancel out. What cannot be removed is a global shock, when markets fall together (see Global financial linkages).

Example: In calendar 2022 the US S&P 500 fell about 19%, while India's Nifty 50 ended the year up about 4%. An investor holding both lost far less than one holding only US shares.

Feasible set of portfolios

The feasible set (or opportunity set) is all the portfolios an investor can make from a given group of securities by changing the weights. When each portfolio is plotted on a graph with risk (σ) on the x-axis and expected return on the y-axis, the points fill an umbrella or bullet-shaped area.

Example: With just two shares A and B, you can hold 100% A, 70% A and 30% B, 50/50, and so on. Every such mix is one point in the feasible set.

Efficient set of portfolios

The efficient set is the part of the feasible set that is worth choosing. A portfolio is efficient if no other portfolio gives a higher return for the same risk, or a lower risk for the same return. This is the dominance principle. Efficient portfolios lie on the upper-left edge of the feasible set, called the efficient frontier.

Example: Portfolio X: 12% return, σ 10%. Portfolio Y: 10% return, σ 10%. Same risk, but X gives more, so X dominates Y and Y is not efficient.

Selection of optimal portfolio

The optimal portfolio is the one efficient portfolio that suits a particular investor best. It depends on the investor's risk appetite, shown by indifference curves (curves of risk-return combinations the investor likes equally). The optimal portfolio is the point where the highest indifference curve touches the efficient frontier.

Example: A retired person picks a low-risk point on the frontier (more bonds). A young aggressive investor picks a high-risk, high-return point (more equity). Both are efficient, but the optimal choice differs.

Markowitz model

Harry Markowitz gave this model in 1952 (Modern Portfolio Theory). It says investors should judge a portfolio by its expected return and risk (variance) together, not each share alone. Inputs are the expected return and σ of each share and the covariance between every pair. Output is the efficient frontier.

Assumptions: investors are rational and dislike risk, and they decide using only return and risk.

Limitation: it needs many inputs. For n shares you need n(n − 1)/2 covariances, so 50 shares need 1,225.

Role of AI in portfolio management

Unit 4 (AI for climate risk, monitoring and rebalancing) and Unit 5 (robo-advisory, algorithmic trading, alternative data) already cover several uses. Other uses in portfolio management:

  • Sentiment analysis: reading news and social media to judge market mood.
  • Optimisation: working out the efficient frontier for hundreds of shares in seconds, which Markowitz's model needs.

Example: An AI tool scans thousands of headlines about a company each day. When the news suddenly turns negative, it alerts the fund manager to review the share before the price falls.
